The Annotated Handsome Frank

Franklin Pierce, 14th President of the United States

“I hope he won't be elected for I should not like to be at Washington and I know you would not either.”

Benjamin Pierce, age 11, to his mother, 1852

The premise. Franklin Pierce was the most likeable man ever to hold the office and one of the worst presidents to hold it. Nobody asked what he stood for. They nominated him on the 49th ballot because almost nobody knew who he was, and eight weeks before he took office his last living son was killed in front of him. This album is his story, told by him, in the order it happened.

The method. Every historical claim in these twelve songs traces to the documented record. Pierce collects more legends than most presidents, so this album flags them on the record: the famous drunk quote is fake and track 10 denies it in his own voice; his wife wrote one letter to their dead son, not years of them; and Handsome Frank is our title, not anything he was called in his lifetime. The sound is invented. The rest happened.

I · The likeable man

01

They Liked Me

The first fact about him, and the reason for everything after. Nathaniel Hawthorne, his closest friend, wrote that his face made “a kind of sunshine” no shyness could resist, in a campaign biography written to get him elected. As a lawyer he read juries, not precedent. The New Hampshire House made him Speaker at 26 (1831), and the U.S. Senate took him at 32, the youngest man then sitting, which is not the same as youngest ever, and the song corrects the common error on purpose. The bridge admits the title is ours: “that is not what they called me… what they called me was likeable.”

⚑ “Handsome Frank” has no 1850s citation. It is our title for him, never presented as his nickname.
02

The Fainting General

Contreras, outside Mexico City, 19 August 1847. His horse reared on the lava rock and fell, throwing him onto the pommel; it rolled onto his knee and he was knocked senseless. A remark reported from his own brigade called him a coward. Five years later the Whig papers turned the fall into a brand, the Fainting General, and printed a joke book, The Military Services of General Pierce, made tiny on purpose. The Richmond Whig wrote of “his known propensity for fainting.” He was unconscious in a lava field. They set it in type as a character flaw.

03

The Way Out Was Me

Baltimore, June 1852. The Democrats needed two-thirds and four big men split the room: Cass, Buchanan, Marcy, Douglas. Thirty-four ballots, not a single vote for Pierce. Virginia put his name up on the 35th, and he was nominated on the 49th with 282 votes. He won because he had, in the Miller Center's phrase, “few enemies, nor indeed any reputation.” They did not pick a man. They picked the way out.

⚑ Some sources say the 48th ballot. The convention record says 49.

II · The wreck and the silence

04

I Heard the Million

He wrote that the use of his name would be “utterly repugnant to my tastes and wishes,” and in the same letter that his life had been “so barren of usefulness.” Both sentences are his (to Charles Atherton, January 1852). Two votes against him at his own table: his son Benny, eleven, wrote “I hope he won't be elected,” and his wife Jane, who had already buried two sons, wished him “out of political life.” In November he beat his own former commanding general, Winfield Scott, 27 states to 4. They were to go home in January to pack.

05

The Little Boy

6 January 1853, about one in the afternoon, a Boston & Maine train three miles above Andover. An axle broke and the car went fifteen or twenty feet down an embankment into a rocky field. About sixty people were aboard and several were hurt. Benny, eleven, was the only one killed. The song lets a contemporary newspaper say it, because nothing better has been written: “Gen. Pierce took him up, he did not think the little boy was dead until he took off his cap.” They buried him from Jane's sister's house. Eight weeks later Pierce was sworn in.

⚑ The cause of the axle failure is not settled, and whether Benny stood at the window is contested. The song says only what is known.
06

She Wore It Anyway

Jane Means Appleton Pierce was raised in strict Calvinism, a minister's daughter who believed nothing is wasted. The scholarly reading of her grief is “providential clearing”: that God had taken Benny so her husband could do the work he had wanted without distraction. She did not attend the inauguration and wrote on black-edged mourning paper. The song gives Pierce the hardest line on the record: she was right about the trade and wrong about who made it. An axle broke. But he had wanted the office enough to write a letter saying he didn't.

⚑ That she called it punishment is a historians' characterization; no sentence of hers says so. The song never uses the word for her.
07

I Said I Affirm

4 March 1853, snow coming sideways, no inaugural ball, Jane not in the city. He is the only president to affirm the oath rather than swear it, and the first to give his inaugural address entirely from memory: about 3,329 words, no notes. His standard biographer found no evidence of why he affirmed, and the song refuses to supply one. The outro is his real opening line: “It is a relief to feel that no heart but my own can know the personal regret and bitter sorrow over which I have been borne to a position so suitable for others rather than desirable for myself.”

⚑ “He refused the Bible because he blamed God” and “hand on a law book” are unsourced. Neither is on the record.
08

The Unsent Letter

Three weeks after the wreck, Jane wrote to Benny, already buried: “My precious child, I must write to you although you are never to see it or know it.” The letter is held by the New Hampshire Historical Society, and its lines in the song are hers: “Oh Benny, I have not valued such a sweet blessing as I ought.” The famous version says she wrote to him for years. The catalogue holds one letter. Then she carried it instead, ten years in black, and on their travels abroad after the presidency she carried Benny's Bible the whole way.

⚑ “She wrote to her dead son for years” is legend. One letter is catalogued; the song says one.

III · The damage

09

The Question Is at Rest

In his inaugural he said he “fervently hope[d] that the question is at rest”, meaning slavery in the territories. Fourteen months later, in May 1854, he signed the Kansas–Nebraska Act, which repealed the Missouri Compromise line he had called settled. He did not write the bill but encouraged it, and would rather have sent the question to the Supreme Court. Jane opposed it and urged him to veto it. What his signature bought: the Whigs died, the Republicans were born, Kansas filled with men who came to vote with guns, Lawrence burned, and a lawyer in Springfield, Illinois went back into politics. The New Hampshire Historical Society calls it “his most significant failure.”

⚑ Jane's position is documented. The two words “Veto it” are that position put into speech, not a quotation.
10

Easier as a Joke

Cincinnati, June 1856. His own party balloted him away; he withdrew before the 15th ballot and Buchanan took it on the 17th. He is the only elected president who sought his party's renomination and was refused (Tyler, Fillmore, Andrew Johnson and Arthur were turned down too, but none had been elected to the office). Then the drinking. He joined the Congressional Temperance Society in 1837, quit in 1841, and in 1843 chaired the meeting whose petition of 1,700-plus signatures made Concord dry. The line everybody quotes, about nothing being left to do after the White House but get drunk, he denies in his own voice. He is right to.

⚑ “There's nothing left to do but get drunk” is legend: no primary source, unstable wording. So is the 1853 drunk-carriage arrest.
11

An Old Friend Stands

Nathaniel Hawthorne, Bowdoin, one class behind him: a shy boy from Salem who was his friend before there was anything to get. In 1852 Hawthorne wrote the campaign biography. His brother-in-law Horace Mann said that if it made Pierce great or brave it would be “the greatest work of fiction he ever wrote,” and Hawthorne said it cost him “hundreds of friends, here at the north.” Pierce gave him the Liverpool consulship. In 1863, with Pierce despised, Hawthorne dedicated his last finished book, Our Old Home, to him over his publisher's objection: “it would be a piece of poltroonery in me to withdraw either the dedication or the dedicatory letter.” He died in May 1864, travelling with Pierce.

12

Render Your Verdict

He died in Concord on 8 October 1869. Grant gave a day of national mourning, and little was written. A statue was proposed in 1888 and blocked for decades by veterans and New Hampshire Republicans who called him a traitor: his 1860 letter to Jefferson Davis had been seized and published, and a broadside built on a forged letter tied him to the Knights of the Golden Circle. In 1911 legislators were booing and hissing each other over his likeness. It was dedicated on 25 November 1914, just under 45 years after his death, once the men who would not forgive him had died. He is buried in Concord's Old North Cemetery beside Jane and two of his sons. The title comes from a historian's article on the fight: “History has rendered its verdict upon him.”

⚑ The broadside is real; the charge inside it came from a fabricated letter. The song says half of it was forged.

No recording

There is no recording of Franklin Pierce's voice, and there could not be. He died in 1869; recorded sound arrived about eight years later.

Album one ended on the oldest surviving recording of a president, Benjamin Harrison on a wax cylinder. This album ends without one, on purpose. Its closing argument is the absence.

How this record was made

Rushmore Rejects is a music project of River Road Labs, a one-person software-and-art studio. The research, lyrics and album architecture are human-written; the vocals and instrumentation were produced with AI music tools from those lyrics, performed in a custom voice built for the character, never derived from any president's real voice; the visuals are AI-generated from public-domain portraiture of the real Franklin Pierce. The AI-generated content disclosure is filed with our distributor on every track. History's melodies are reconstructed; history's facts are not.

Corrections welcome.
