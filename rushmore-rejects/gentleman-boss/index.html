The Annotated Gentleman Boss

Chester Alan Arthur, 21st President of the United States

“No man ever entered the Presidency so profoundly and widely distrusted as Chester Alan Arthur, and no one ever retired from the highest civil trust of the world more generally respected, alike by political friend and foe.”

Alexander K. McClure, who watched it all

The premise. Chester Alan Arthur was the 21st President of the United States, and you have never thought about him once. He was a machine politician handed the presidency by an assassin who shouted his name, and he answered by betraying the machine that made him. No recording of his voice survives. This album is what happens if history had kept one, and it had bars.

The method. Every historical claim in these twelve songs traces to the documented record. Where history is legend rather than fact, the lyrics say “they say.” Where a famous quote can't be sourced, we left it out. The one thing on this record that is invented is the sound. The rest happened.

The twelve, annotated

01

The Collector

deep house

In 1871 Grant made Arthur Collector of the Port of New York, the choke point where roughly three-quarters of the nation's customs revenue landed, with some 1,300 jobs in his gift. The law gave the Collector a “moiety,” a legal cut of fines on cheating importers, which pushed his income past the President's $50,000. When Congress abolished moieties in 1874 his pay fell to about $12,000, and he stayed. Here is the part that reads like fiction: committees combed his books for years and never traced a stolen dollar to him. The machine was crooked; his ledgers were clean. That paradox is the album.

02

Machine

boom bap

Before the machine: in 1854, two months into practicing law, the junior man got the file nobody wanted. Elizabeth Jennings, a Black schoolteacher, had been thrown off a Third Avenue streetcar. Arthur won her $225 plus costs in a Brooklyn courtroom in February 1855, and the verdict broke the colour line on New York's streetcars. Then he met Senator Roscoe Conkling and mastered the spoils system. The skit at the end is verbatim from a Delmonico's dinner, February 1881, where the crowd chanted “SOAP!” (slang for vote-buying cash) while Arthur joked about “perfect organization” in Indiana. Reporters were present. He noticed.

03

Fired by Hayes

diss track

The beef is real. Rutherford B. Hayes lost the popular vote in 1876 and took the presidency 8–7 on a party-line Electoral Commission, earning the nicknames “His Fraudulency” and “Rutherfraud,” coined by others, and the song is careful to say so. Hayes' Jay Commission audited Arthur's Customs House and found no dollar traced to him, so Hayes offered him the Paris consulship to leave quietly. Arthur declined. Hayes waited for a Senate recess in July 1878 and swept his desk. The kill shot is true and merciless: Edison's phonograph was demonstrated at the Hayes White House in 1878, and no recording of Hayes' voice survives either. They recorded him. Nobody kept it.

04

Gentleman Boss

the single

The flexes are documented: “the Gentleman Boss” and “Elegant Arthur” were his actual nicknames, and the word “dude” was coined in January 1883 and promptly applied to him: the Dude President, first of the name. The eighty pairs of trousers are legend, and the song sings them as legend (“they printed… I'll allow it”). The “born in Canada” smear by lawyer Arthur Hinman was America's first birther conspiracy, false; he was born in Vermont. He did shave a year off his age for the papers. Mark Twain, no friend of presidents: “it would be hard indeed to better President Arthur's administration.”

05

Accidental

dark trap

2 July 1881, Baltimore & Potomac station: Charles Guiteau, a delusional office-seeker who had begged for the same Paris consulship Arthur once turned down, shot President Garfield and announced, “I am a Stalwart of the Stalwarts!… Arthur is President now!” He had said Arthur's name. For 79 days Garfield lay dying while doctors probed the wound with unwashed fingers, and the papers measured Arthur for a villain's suit. A staffer told reporters the truth: he was “sitting alone in his room sobbing like a child.” The oath came at 2:15 a.m. on 20 September, in his own parlour at 123 Lexington Avenue. The a cappella line that closes the track is Arthur, verbatim, two days later: “Men may die, but the fabrics of our free institutions remain unshaken.”

06

Dear Chester

the duet

Julia Sand was a bedridden invalid on East 74th Street who wrote the new President twenty-three letters between August 1881 and September 1883: scolding, coaching, believing. Her lines in this song are her lines: “Great emergencies awaken generous traits which have lain dormant half a life”; “making a man President CAN change him”; and the album's golden sentence, “It is for you to choose whether your record shall be written in black or in gold.” She signed herself his “little dwarf.” He never wrote back. But on 20 August 1882, a Sunday, unannounced, the President's carriage stopped at her house and he sat in her parlour for an hour. When everything else burned, her letters are among the papers that survived. She also left history its only description of his voice: “a gentle-voiced Episcopalian minister.” She kept his voice.

07

Pendleton

piano house · the pitch

16 January 1883: the machine's own man signed the Pendleton Civil Service Act: competitive exams, merit hiring, and a ban on the salary assessments that had funded his entire political life. The song calls it “the death certificate of everything that made me,” which is just accurate. He then enforced it honestly: Dorman Eaton chaired the new commission, and the Star Route prosecutions went after his own faction. The mock-gospel bridge is a real Stalwart lament, sung here exactly as recorded: “He has done less for us than Garfield, or even Hayes.” The Conkling coda is all true: the declined Supreme Court seat, confirmed 39–12, the last person in American history to decline a confirmed seat, and the 1888 Blizzard death, walking three miles rather than pay a cabman's fare.

08

Exclusion

the reckoning

The album stops flexing here, because the record demands it. In April 1882 Arthur vetoed a twenty-year Chinese exclusion bill, but read his veto: he called it “a breach of our national faith” because it broke a treaty and threatened “the trade of the East.” Not because of what it did to people. Congress shaved twenty years to ten, and on 6 May 1882 he signed the Chinese Exclusion Act, the first federal law to bar a people by race. That August he signed the head-tax act with its “convict, lunatic, idiot” language. The final verse sets the two papers side by side: the young lawyer who desegregated New York's streetcars, and the President who closed the shore, the same signed name. The song refuses to invent private anguish for him: “The record shows no anguish. The record shows I signed.”

09

Steel Navy

big room

The Navy he inherited was rotting wood. On 3 March 1883 he signed the authorization for the first steel warships of the modern American fleet, the ABCD ships: Atlanta, Boston, Chicago, Dolphin, built at John Roach's yard on the Delaware. The bridge is the poignant true detail: the White Squadron sailed together in 1889. Arthur died in 1886. He built the ships and never saw them sail. Build them anyway.

10

Tiffany Glass

luxury rap

“I will not live in a house like this”, verbatim, on inspecting the White House. He sold twenty-four wagonloads of historic clutter at public auction, roughly 5,000 spectators; the rat-traps sold; every dollar reached the Treasury. Then he brought in Louis Comfort Tiffany, 34, to install some 338 square feet of jewelled glass in the entrance hall. The receipts in the last verse are real: standard time zones, November 1883; the Brooklyn Bridge opening, May 1883; the Washington Monument capstone, December 1884. And the screen? Theodore Roosevelt took a sledgehammer renovation to it in 1902. Nothing gilded survived but the law.

11

Nell

the ballad

Ellen “Nell” Herndon Arthur, a captain's daughter and a genuinely gifted singer with the Mendelssohn Glee Club, caught cold waiting for a carriage after a benefit concert and died of pneumonia on 12 January 1880, at 42. Arthur was in Albany on machine business; he reached her but she never knew it. Twenty months later he was President. The rituals in verse two are documented: fresh flowers before her portrait every morning; the stained-glass window he had installed on the south side of St. John's Church, so he could see it lit at night from the White House; the hostess chair his sister filled while the place beside him stayed hers. The hook is his sentence, verbatim: “Honors to me now are not what they once were.”

12

Burn the Papers

the exit

Diagnosed with Bright's disease, then a death sentence, in 1882, he told no one; the White House blamed malaria and the papers called him lazy while he was dying on schedule. He let his name go forward in 1884 only so no one could say he ran scared, took 207 delegates, lost to Blaine, and by one account never slept better. Asked what an ex-President might do: “there doesn't seem anything else for an ex-President to do but to go into the country and raise big pumpkins.” He was serious. On 16 November 1886, at 123 Lexington, the same parlour where he took the oath, he had three garbage cans, four feet high, filled with his papers and burned. The Collector's books, the machine's receipts, whatever might explain him. Julia Sand's letters survived. A hemorrhage came the next day; he died 18 November, age 57, and was buried beside Nell in Albany.

The last sound on the record

The final sound on this album is real. After the last verse, rising out of the crackle, you hear Benjamin Harrison, the oldest surviving recording of any American president's voice, preserved on a wax cylinder and in the public domain.

Nobody is certain what year it was cut: sources put it anywhere from his term to several years after it. A president's voice survived, and we lost the date. It plays at the end of an album about the president history didn't keep.

How this record was made

Rushmore Rejects is a music project of River Road Labs, a one-person software-and-art studio. The research, lyrics and album architecture are human-written; the vocals and instrumentation were produced with AI music tools from those lyrics, performed in a custom voice built for the character; the visuals are AI-generated from public-domain portraiture of the real Chester A. Arthur. The AI-generated content disclosure is filed with our distributor on every track. History's melodies are reconstructed; history's facts are not.
